Australian public libraries · 2010–2025

Fewer cards, more clicks

Australians hold fewer library cards and walk through library doors less often than a decade ago. Yet libraries are busier than ever, because borrowing has moved online and the buildings now do far more than lend books.

1 The long slide

Through the 2010s, Australians borrowed a little less from their libraries every year. By 2019 the average person was borrowing two fewer items a year than in 2011, and visiting less often too.

The rankings barely moved: South Australia and Tasmania borrowed the most per person every year, and the Northern Territory the least. Over the decade, Tasmania and the ACT fell furthest.

2 The crash

COVID-19 lockdowns closed library doors across the country. In-person visits fell from 111 million in 2018–19 to 56 million in 2021–22, while visits to library websites held steady.

Membership kept falling after the lockdowns ended. Libraries say part of this is housekeeping: inactive cards expired during closures, and some services moved to family cards.

3 A digital recovery

Total borrowing has now passed its pre-COVID level, but not because people returned to the shelves. Physical loans are still 13% below 2018–19. All of the growth is digital: ebook, audiobook and streaming loans nearly tripled in six years.

The shift is fastest in the ACT and Queensland, where around two in every five loans are now digital. South Australia is the only state where the digital share fell.

4 Where libraries are used most

Library use varies widely across the country. South Australians borrow three times as many items per person as Territorians, and the Northern Territory has the most branches per person because its towns are small and far apart.

5 More than books, on less money

Libraries now run more than 430,000 programs a year, from baby rhyme time to tech help for seniors, and attendance has doubled since 2021–22. Meanwhile, funding per person has fallen 13% in real terms over five years.

What it means

The library card is no longer the best measure of who uses a library. Australians are borrowing on their phones, studying, meeting and bringing their children to story time, often without ever scanning a card. The open question is whether funding will keep pace with that broader role.
